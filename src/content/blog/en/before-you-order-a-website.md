Almost no website project runs late because of the programming. It runs late because everyone is
waiting on the text. Or the photos. Or because halfway through, somebody in the company says they
actually wanted something else.

The list below is what I ask for at the start of every project. If you have these ready before you
request your first quote, you get a more accurate price, a faster delivery, and a site that actually
resembles what you had in mind.

## 1. One goal, stated in numbers

"I want a more modern site" is not a goal, it is a mood. "I want at least five enquiries a month from
the site" is a goal — and it completely changes how the page gets built.

A site built for enquiries looks different from one built to boost your credibility before a tender,
which looks different from one built to cut down repetitive phone calls. Pick a main one. The others
can be secondary, but only one drives the decisions.

## 2. Who your customer is, specifically

Not "everyone". Describe the person who lands on the site: what they are looking for, what worries
them, what would make them close the tab. If you sell accounting services to a five-person company,
the message differs from one aimed at corporations.

The most useful thing you can give me here: **the questions clients ask you on the phone**. Those
almost always become sections on the site.

## 3. The text — or the decision that someone else writes it

This is where most projects stall. People underestimate how long it takes to write about their own
company, and postpone indefinitely.

You have two honest options: either set aside two or three hours and write a rough first version
yourself — it does not need to be pretty, only accurate and complete — or decide upfront that someone
else writes it and put that in the budget. The third option, "I will send it next week", is the one
that stretches projects into months.

## 4. Photos at proper resolution

You will need: pictures of you or your team, of your premises or your work, and of products where
relevant. Taken on a phone in good light, these are almost always better than stock photography of
people in suits shaking hands.

Send them at original resolution, not through a messaging app — those compress heavily, and that
quality cannot be recovered.

If you have no photos at all, say so at the start. Carefully chosen stock photography can work, but
that is a decision made together, not a surprise discovered halfway through.

## 5. Logo and visual identity

Ideally: your logo in vector format (`.svg`, `.ai` or `.eps`) and your colour codes. If all you have
is a `.jpg` from an old business card, say so — it can be redrawn, but that is a separate job.

If you have no logo at all, that is fine and you do not need to commission one before the site. A
clean typographic treatment works.

## 6. The domain and its login, if you already have one

If you own a domain, you need access to the account it was bought from. It sounds trivial, but it is
a frequent source of delay: the domain was bought six years ago by an acquaintance who "was good with
computers", and nobody remembers which email the account is on.

Check this now, not on launch day. Recovering a domain can take weeks.

## 7. Three sites you like, and why

Not necessarily from your industry. What matters is telling me **why** you like each one: "I like how
uncluttered it is", "I like that you understand what they do in five seconds", "I like the colour".

One concrete example with a short explanation saves two rounds of design revisions. An example of a
site you dislike, with the same kind of reason, is worth just as much.

## 8. One person who decides

If feedback arrives from three people with different opinions, the project goes in circles. Somebody
has to have the final word and use it.

That does not mean others cannot comment — it means feedback arrives filtered, in one voice, rather
than in three emails that contradict each other.

## What this actually saves you

A project where content arrives on time takes two to three weeks. The same project, with text
trickling in, frequently reaches two months — not because there is more work, but because every pause
means starting up again.

You do not need all of this perfect before the first conversation. But if points 1, 2 and 7 are
clear, that conversation is much shorter and the quote you get lands much closer to the final price.
