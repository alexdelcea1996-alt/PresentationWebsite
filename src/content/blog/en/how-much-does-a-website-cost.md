You asked three people to quote for a business website and got three numbers that do not seem to
describe the same thing. That is normal, and it is not because anyone is lying to you. "Business
website" is not a product with a specification. It is a category, like "car".

Here is what sits behind the difference, and the questions that make it go away.

## First: what you pay once, and what you pay every year

Almost every misunderstanding starts here. These are two separate sums and they get mixed into the
same conversation.

**Once:** the build itself. Page structure, design, writing or reworking the text, getting the
photographs into shape, the contact form, putting it on a server.

**Every year:** the domain name and the hosting. Hosting varies enormously — a well-built static
site can sit free on serious infrastructure, while a WordPress site with a shop needs a paid plan
every month.

If a quote does not separate the two clearly, ask for it. Not because anything is being hidden, but
because you can only compare like with like afterwards.

## Why quotes differ: three different roads

**A bought theme, filled in.** You take a ready-made template, change the colours and the words. It
is the cheapest road and for some businesses it is entirely sufficient. The low price is real. What
you pay instead: the site looks like several thousand others, and commercial themes carry, as a
rule, a great deal of code you have no use for — which is most of [why sites load
slowly](/en/blog/why-your-site-is-slow/).

**Built to order by a person.** The structure starts from what you sell rather than from what the
template allowed. It costs more because somebody spends days thinking about your page in particular.

**An agency.** The same work, with more people involved and a coordination cost on top. Sometimes
worth it — large projects need it. For a five-page site, usually not.

None of these is "correct". They are three answers to three different situations.

## What actually moves the price

Not the number of pages, which is what everyone assumes. Adding a sixth page to a site that has five
is a few hours of work.

What moves it, in order:

1. **Content that does not exist yet.** If the words and the photographs have to be made from
   scratch, that is a separate part of the project and often the longest one. I wrote about [what to
   prepare in advance](/en/blog/before-you-order-a-website/) precisely to shrink it.
2. **Features that leave "pages with text" behind.** Bookings, calculators, user accounts, a link to
   your invoicing software. Each one is a small application living inside the site.
3. **The number of languages.** It does not double the price, but it is not free either: every page,
   every button and every error message exists twice and has to stay in step.
4. **How much thinking you ask for up front.** A site that starts from "make it like my competitor's"
   costs less than one that starts from a conversation about who your customers are. The second
   brings in more.

## Five questions that make two quotes comparable

Send them to both. The difference between the answers tells you more than the number at the bottom.

1. **What happens to the price if I ask for a change at the third revision?** Look for an answer that
   states plainly what is included and what is billed separately.
2. **Whose name are the domain and the hosting in?** The only good answer is "yours". More on that in
   [who actually owns your website](/en/blog/who-actually-owns-your-website/).
3. **What do I get if I want to work with somebody else tomorrow?** The code, the accounts, access to
   the content.
4. **Who writes the words?** If the answer is "you", that is a part of the project you have just
   taken on, and it is not in the price.
5. **How does the site perform on a phone — measured, not described?** Ask for a report, not a
   promise. A [PageSpeed Insights](https://pagespeed.web.dev) run on a site they have already built
   is enough.

## What is almost never included, in any quote

Professional copywriting, product photography, translation by an actual translator, advertising
campaigns, and content added every month. Not because they are hidden — because they are different
trades. If your budget has to cover those too, say so at the start and we build around the reality
rather than around a plan that breaks in month two.
