"How much does an online store cost?" is a question where any short answer is false. Not because the
price is a secret, but because the question mixes three different things: what you pay to have it,
what you pay to keep it, and what leaves with every order.

Here they are one at a time, with the figures I can stand behind. Where I do not have a figure, I say
so.

## What you pay once: the build

A custom-built store starts **from €2,200** and is delivered in **4–6 weeks**.

Briefly, what that covers: a catalogue with products, variants and stock; a short checkout with the
delivery cost visible early; online payments; automatic invoicing; courier labels generated for you;
customer accounts; product pages prepared for Google; and the structure of the legal documents you
are required to have.

The price rises with the number of products, how many variants each one has, the integrations
required and — very often — the content: who writes the descriptions and who takes the photographs.
It does not rise for "premium design"; the design is included, not sold as a separate tier.

## What you pay on every order

This is the part quotes tend to leave unsaid, and the only one that grows as you do.

**The payment processor's commission.** Generally **between 1% and 2.5% per transaction**, depending
which one you choose. The money lands in your company's account, usually one to three working days
after the order. That commission goes to the processor — not to me, and not to any middleman between
the two of you.

**Delivery.** It depends on the courier, the weight, the region and your monthly volume; rates are
negotiated and differ from company to company, so any number I put here would be an invention. What
matters for the store is not the rate but that it **appears early in the checkout**: most carts are
abandoned because shipping shows up as a surprise at the end.

**Invoicing.** If you already use SmartBill, Oblio or FGO, you stay on the subscription you have —
the store connects to it. If you use nothing today, that is a new cost, at whichever provider you
pick.

**Domain and hosting.** They exist and need budgeting, but they are invoices from your providers, not
from me.

## What you do not pay

**Commission on sales, to me: 0%.** The store is yours and I take no percentage of what you sell.
That is the fundamental difference from a marketplace, where the commission on every sale is the
business model.

**A mandatory subscription.** Once the included support period ends, you owe nothing to keep the
store running. Later changes are billed hourly or on a monthly contract if you want one — but that is
a choice, not a condition.

**The code and the accounts** are in your name from day one. If you work with somebody else
tomorrow, you take everything and go.

## Why quotes differ so much

If you asked for three quotes and got three numbers that look unrelated, it is usually not bad faith.
It is that the three quotes describe different projects. What separates them, almost always:

- **How many products and how many variants.** A store with 30 products and no variants and one with
  3,000 SKUs are not the same project, however similar the home page looks.
- **What gets integrated.** Payments, invoicing, couriers, e-invoicing, an existing ERP — every
  connection is work, and every one is a source of later trouble if it is done badly.
- **Who writes the content.** Product descriptions are the most underestimated line in the budget.
- **What happens after delivery.** A quote with support included and one without are not directly
  comparable.

## When you do not need a store

Worth saying, because it is the cheapest advice in this article.

If you **sell a few products occasionally**, a business website with an order form is cheaper and
enough. If you are **still testing whether the product sells**, start on a marketplace, where you
have traffic from day one — your own store makes sense once you have steady sales and the commission
starts to hurt. And if you do **dropshipping with thousands of automatically imported products**, you
need a different architecture from the one I build.

Your own store pays for itself out of the margin you stop handing to somebody else. If you do not yet
have the sales to pay for it, it is an expense, not an investment.

## Five questions for comparing two quotes

1. **What commission do you take on each sale?** (If the answer is not "none", ask how much and on
   what.)
2. **Whose name are the code, the domain and the payment accounts in?**
3. **What happens after delivery — how much support is included, and what does it cost afterwards?**
4. **Who writes the product descriptions and who takes the photographs?**
5. **What happens if I want to move to another developer?**

The last one is the most useful. An evasive answer there tells you more about the next two years than
any number in the quote.
