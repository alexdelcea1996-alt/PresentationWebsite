The question comes up in almost every first conversation, and it is usually phrased as a budget
problem: "is one page enough, or do I need a whole site?" But the budget is the last part of the
answer, not the first. A landing page and a business website are not the small and large versions of
the same thing. They are two tools that solve different problems, and the wrong one is not cheaper —
it is simply unfinished.

## The difference, in one sentence

A **landing page** converts people you bring. A **business website** gets you found by people you
have never met.

The rest of this article is what follows from that.

## Where the people come from

This is the criterion that decides, and it is surprisingly often ignored.

If your traffic **comes from a source you control** — paid ads, an email list, a QR code on a poster,
a post you paid to boost — the visitor arrives already knowing roughly what you offer. They do not
need to explore. They need one short path to the action. That is a landing page.

If you want traffic to **arrive on its own, from search** — somebody types "accountant in Cluj" or
"plumbing company Timișoara" and you want to be found — then you need several pages, each about one
thing. A single page will not rank for dozens of different searches, however well it is written.
That is a business website.

The practical test: **if you switch the ads off, does anyone still land on the page?** If the answer
is "no, and that is fine, the campaign is temporary" — landing page. If the answer is "no, and that
is exactly my problem" — business website.

## What each one actually does

A landing page is built around a single decision. It has no menu, because a menu is a list of ways
for the visitor to leave the page you paid to get them onto. It has one button, repeated, and a short
form. Anything that does not serve the decision comes out — including true, good things about your
company.

A business website does the opposite: it covers your subjects. A page per service, a page about who
you are, a form, perhaps a blog. Each page can be found separately in Google and can be the entry
point for a different person with a different question.

One thing worth saying plainly: **a business website needs pages that convert, too.** It is not a
brochure. But its structure is built to cover several intentions rather than one, and every other
difference follows from that.

## Cost and time

| Criterion | Landing page | Business website |
|---|---|---|
| Starting price | from €400 | from €900 |
| Delivery | ~1 week | 2–4 weeks |
| Included afterwards | 30 days of adjustments | 60 days of support |

The price difference does not come from "more pages". It comes from the fact that a business website
needs a structure built for search, copy for each service, and decisions about how the site grows
over the next few years. A landing page needs one decision, made very well.

The difference in **time** often matters more than the difference in price. If you are launching a
course in ten days, the conversation about a full website is theoretical: you do not have time for
one, but you do have time for a page.

## Three questions that decide it

1. **Who sends people to the page — you or Google?** You → landing page. Google → website.
2. **How many different things are you selling?** One, right now → landing page. Several services,
   each with its own customer → website.
3. **How long does the offer last?** A campaign, an event, a launch → landing page. Something you
   will still be selling in two years → website.

If the answers disagree, the last one wins. A permanent tool deserves to be built as one.

## The mistake that costs the most

It is not picking the "wrong" one of the two. It is paying for ads and sending people **to your home
page**.

A home page is built to serve anyone who arrives for any reason. Someone who clicked an ad about one
specific thing lands in a menu with eight entries and a general message, and has to go looking for
the thing they came for. Some of them do not go looking. You paid for the click either way.

This is where a landing page pays for itself fastest: not because it is cheap, but because you stop
throwing away part of the ad budget.

## The "both" case

It is more common than it sounds, and it is not an attempt to sell you two things.

You have a business website that does its job in search, and you start a campaign for one particular
service. You do not distort the site to make the campaign fit inside it; you build a separate page,
point the ads at it, measure it, and when the campaign ends the page either stays or disappears
without anything else being touched.

The usual order, if you are starting from nothing and the budget is limited: **first the tool that
brings money now**. Usually that is the landing page, if you already have somewhere to bring traffic
from. The business website is the investment that pays back in months rather than days — which does
not make it optional, only second.

## When neither is the answer

If you sell several products with variants and stock, this is not your question — you need an online
store, which is a different category of project.

And if you have **nowhere to bring traffic from** — no ads, no list, no audience — a new page changes
nothing. That is not a website problem; it is a distribution problem, and it gets solved before, not
after.
