The ugliest conversations I have with people who want a new site all start the same way: "I would
like to change something, but I cannot reach whoever built it."

Sometimes that is sorted in an hour. Sometimes the person discovers that their domain — the one their
email address on every invoice is built on — is registered in somebody else's name.

It is not necessarily bad faith. Most of the time it is convenience: it was simpler for the developer
to buy everything on their own account. The outcome is the same.

## A website has four possible owners, not one

These are four separate things. They can sit in four different names, and often do.

**The domain** — the address. Rented yearly from a registrar.

**The hosting** — the server the files live on. A different account, often with a different company.

**The code and the content** — the files themselves, the words, the photographs, the database.

**The accounts around it** — Google Analytics, Google Search Console, the Google Business Profile,
the email addresses, the payment processor account.

The fourth is the one most often forgotten, and the one that hurts most two years later, when you
want to see how traffic has changed and cannot get into anything.

## Check today, in ten minutes

**The domain.** Go to [who.is](https://who.is) and look up your address. Look at the registrant. If
it says your company, good. If it says somebody else, or a web design firm, you have found something
to fix. The details may be hidden for privacy reasons — in that case ask directly and ask for proof.

**The hosting.** Do *you* have login details for the control panel? Not "he sends them when I need
them" — you, now, in your hands.

**The Google accounts.** Sign in to [Google Search
Console](https://search.google.com/search-console) with your own email address. If you cannot see
your site there, somebody else can.

**The code.** Ask where it lives and ask for a copy. A good answer sounds like "it is in a Git
repository, I will give you access." An answer that dodges the question is the answer.

## What to ask for before you sign anything

Four sentences. They fit in an email.

1. **The domain is registered in my name or my company's, from day one.** Not "transferred at the
   end". Day one.
2. **I receive the hosting credentials on delivery.** Even if I have no use for them.
3. **The code is mine and I receive a complete copy.** Including whatever is needed to put it on a
   different server.
4. **Every account created for the project is on my email address, with the developer added as a
   user.** Not the other way round.

Nobody serious takes offence at that list. Somebody who works properly offers it before you ask. I
have it written into the [guarantees published on the front page](/en/#guarantees), precisely so it
does not depend on my memory.

## "But if they do not pay, I do not want them walking off with the site"

That is a legitimate worry on the developer's side, and it has a solution that does not involve
holding your domain.

The normal solution is staged payment. Part up front, part on delivery. The site sits on a test
address until the final payment, then it moves. Ownership has nothing to do with it — a carpenter
does not hold the deeds to your house until you pay for the kitchen.

If somebody explains why it is "simpler" for the domain to be in their name, you have your answer. It
is not a good one.

## If you have found that you do not own something

Do not panic and do not go in fighting. Most of the time a polite email settles it.

**For the domain:** ask for a transfer to an account in your name. Every registrar has the procedure,
it takes a few days and usually involves an authorisation code.

**For Google accounts:** ask to be added as an owner, then remove the other one. You can do that
yourself from the interface once you have the role.

**For the code:** ask for an archive. If you do not get one, you still have the content — the words
and the pictures are yours and can be recovered from the public site.

**If you get no reply at all:** the registrar has a procedure for exactly this. Have your evidence
ready that your company paid for the domain: invoices, statements.

## Why I keep pressing on this

Because it is the one part of a web project where the mistake stays invisible for years and then
cannot be fixed cheaply. An ugly design gets replaced. A slow site gets optimised. A lost domain that
you built a reputation on, and that the email address on every invoice depends on, is a different
conversation.
